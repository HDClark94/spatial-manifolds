## Summary

Spatial firing of neurons in the medial entorhinal cortex (MEC) is not fixed to the external world. During a location memory task on a virtual linear track, the firing of a single neuron can be anchored to the task reference frame on some trials and not on others, and this anchoring predicts whether the animal localises a reward that can only be found by path integration. What governs the switching is unknown. Here we show that anchoring is a property of the population rather than of single cells, and that the population state is not built by the entorhinal circuit that expresses it. Using four-shank Neuropixels recordings from 8 mice, with open-field recordings on the same days so that cell identity is assigned independently of the task, we find that trial-by-trial anchoring is correlated across simultaneously recorded neurons and organised into blocks of tens of trials. Measured against each cell's own null, only grid cells and putative interneurons follow this population state; non-grid spatial cells sit at chance. Grid cells and interneurons also agree with each other, and their pairing is confirmed in millisecond spike timing, where interneuron-to-grid is the strongest monosynaptic pathway we detect. Yet synaptically connected pairs share the anchoring state no more than unconnected pairs matched for identity and distance, indicating that the coordination is not propagated through the local circuit. Consistent with the state being imposed from outside, pupil diameter tracks it, and performance improves monotonically as the pupil narrows, identifying the anchored state as one of focused task engagement. Every structure we recorded — including visual cortex and cerebellum — carries a coherent anchoring state of its own, and corrected for how reliably each is estimated these states are indistinguishable. What differs is the cell type rather than the structure. The local field potential changes with the state in a frequency-specific way, with theta and mid gamma falling while slow gamma rises, and theta's coupling to running speed roughly halves — yet the fast-spiking speed cells that carry the septal speed signal, identified in the open field, show no change in their speed coding between states. We suggest that the entorhinal circuit does not generate the anchoring state but contains cell types tuned to read it.

## Introduction

Grid cells in the medial entorhinal cortex (MEC) are thought to provide a metric for space that supports navigation and memory. A standard view is that this metric is continuously available and stably anchored to the external world. Several observations challenge that view. On circular tracks, grid firing remains periodic but is no longer anchored to the environment, so that it is informative about distance travelled rather than position. Populations of MEC neurons can spontaneously remap between representations within a session. During a virtual-track location memory task, grid firing switches between a task-anchored mode, in which fields recur at the same track positions, and a task-independent mode, in which they do not, and this switching is associated with behaviour: anchoring predicts successful localisation of a reward zone when the animal must path-integrate to find it, but not when a visual cue marks it.

That dissociation makes the switching itself worth explaining. If anchoring determines whether an internal position estimate is usable, then whatever controls anchoring controls when the grid code contributes to behaviour. Two broad accounts are available. Under the first, anchoring is set within the entorhinal circuit — by the balance of visual and self-motion input to the grid network, or by interactions among its own neurons, in which case the cells that share an anchoring state should be those that are connected to one another. Under the second, anchoring reflects a variable that is not entorhinal at all, with the circuit responding to a signal generated elsewhere, in which case shared anchoring should track what a cell is rather than what it is wired to, and the same signal should be legible outside MEC.

Distinguishing these accounts requires three things that have not previously been available together: simultaneous recordings of enough neurons to define a population state trial by trial, independent assignment of cell identity so that the categories cannot be shaped by the dynamics under study, and recordings that extend beyond MEC. We obtained these using four-shank Neuropixels 2.0 probes targeted to span the mediolateral extent of MEC, with the same neurons recorded in an open arena on the same day, and with the probe shanks also sampling the pre-, para- and postsubicular complex, visual cortex, and in some animals cerebellum.

We show here that anchoring to the task reference frame is a population-level state that is entered by particular cell types rather than by spatial coding in general: grid cells and putative interneurons follow it, non-grid spatial cells do not. These two classes are synaptically coupled, but their synaptic coupling does not account for their shared anchoring, which indicates that the state is imposed on the population by a common input. Consistent with this, the state tracks pupil-indexed task engagement, and it is present in every structure we recorded, with only the strength of the readout differing by cell type. We conclude that the entorhinal circuit does not construct the anchoring state but contains neurons tuned to it.

## Results

We recorded from four-shank Neuropixels 2.0 probes targeted to sample the mediolateral extent of the MEC, in mice exploring an open arena and then performing a location memory task on a virtual linear track (Figure 1A–C). Mice were trained for 26.6 ± 6.3 days (range 19–41) before recording (Figure 1D). On cued trials the reward zone was marked by a visual cue; on uncued trials the cue was absent and the zone could only be localised efficiently by path integration. Cell identity was assigned from the open-field session: neurons with grid firing fields in the arena ([n]/[n]) typically showed repeating localised firing on the track, whereas neurons with non-grid spatial firing in the arena ([n]/[n]) more often showed ramping profiles (Figure 1E). Across the dataset we analysed 6,455 MEC neurons from 61 sessions in 8 mice.

### Anchoring to the task reference frame is a property of the population

A cell whose firing is anchored on some trials and not others could be switching independently of its neighbours, in which case anchoring is a property of single neurons, or it could be switching together with them, in which case anchoring is a property of the population. These possibilities make different predictions for simultaneously recorded cells: under the first, trial-by-trial anchoring labels should be uncorrelated across cells; under the second, they should be correlated, and the correlation should exceed what is expected from each cell's overall tendency to be anchored.

To distinguish them we classified each trial of each cell as anchored or non-anchored by comparing that trial's rate map against the cell's own circularly shifted null (see Methods). We then took the first principal component of the resulting cell × trial label matrix and compared it against a circular-shift null that preserves each cell's anchored fraction, so that a cell which is anchored on most trials cannot generate apparent population structure on its own. The first principal component exceeded this null by approximately twofold (Figure 1F). Anchoring was organised in time as well as across cells: blocks of tens of trials shared a state, and the population crossed between states over approximately three trials (Figure 1G). We refer to the per-trial fraction of anchored cells as the population anchoring state.

To ask whether this state is behaviourally relevant we compared stopping outcomes between anchored and non-anchored trials. Mice were more likely to stop in the reward zone on anchored trials, and the advantage was larger on uncued trials, where the zone can only be localised by path integration, than on cued trials, where a visual landmark is available. Quantified per session as the difference between the uncued and cued anchoring benefit, which we refer to as the Anchoring Dependence Index, the effect was positive (+0.094, p = 0.012). This reproduces, at the level of a population state, the selective association between anchoring and path integration-dependent performance previously reported for individual grid cells.

Together, these data indicate that anchoring to the task reference frame is a coordinated state of the recorded population rather than an independent property of single neurons, and that it matters most for behaviour when the animal must rely on an internal estimate of position.

### Grid cells and putative interneurons, but not spatial coding in general, follow the population state

The population state is an average over cells and so does not by itself indicate which cells enter it. We considered two possibilities. If anchoring is a property of spatial coding as such, then all spatially tuned cells should follow the state. If instead it is a property of a particular subnetwork, then following should be restricted to specific cell types.

Testing this requires a definition of cell identity that cannot have been shaped by the phenomenon under study. Firing on the linear track changes with engagement and task demand, which is what we are trying to explain, so classifying cells on the same session would allow those dynamics to define the categories and then rediscover them. We therefore assigned identity entirely from the open field — grid, non-grid spatial and non-spatial classes from open-field rate maps — on a different environment and a different behaviour.

It also requires an appropriate null. Chance for this comparison is not zero: a cell that is anchored on most trials will correlate with a population that is also anchored on most trials, and we measured the resulting bias directly by circularly shifting each cell's label sequence. The null sits near +0.15 rather than at zero, and each cell was therefore compared against its own shifted distribution.

Measured this way, only grid cells (mean r = 0.276 against a chance level of 0.157, excess +0.119, p = 0.002, above chance in 6 of 7 mice) and putative interneurons (0.228 against 0.149, excess +0.080, p = 0.0003, 7 of 7 mice) exceeded chance (Figure 2). Non-grid spatial cells sat on their null (excess −0.000, p = 0.33) and non-spatial cells below it (−0.024, p = 0.59). Grid cells and interneurons did not differ from one another (p = 0.84) and both exceeded the other two classes (p ≤ 8 × 10⁻⁷). These group averages are compatible with the presence of individual followers in the other classes: 19–24% of non-grid and non-spatial cells individually beat a 5% false-positive rate, so those populations contain genuine followers within a majority that sits at chance.

That both classes exceed chance does not establish that they are coordinated with each other, since two populations could track a common signal independently. We therefore correlated every pair of simultaneously recorded cells and averaged by identity pair, again as an excess over an independent circular-shift null so that a pair which is simply anchored most of the time scores nothing for it. Grid–interneuron agreement reached +0.109 (29 sessions, p = 1.4 × 10⁻⁶) against +0.058 for the remaining off-diagonal pairs, and was indistinguishable from either within-group value (grid–grid +0.115, interneuron–interneuron +0.115). Aligned to a transition in the population state, all four identities switched together over approximately three trials, and identity did not predict switch timing (p = 0.15).

We carried out three further analyses to establish what a non-anchored trial is. First, a non-anchored trial could reflect a firing field that has moved rather than one that has gone. Correlating each trial's map against the cell's anchored template at every circular offset, the correlation at zero offset collapsed (+0.33 to +0.07) while the best correlation over offsets fell only slightly (+0.56 to +0.53), which superficially suggests displacement. However, two unrelated cells reach +0.500 under the same best-of-offsets search, and non-anchored trials sat only 0.029 above that floor with a mean best offset of 47 cm against the 50 cm expected from a uniform choice on a 200 cm track. Non-anchored trials therefore show loss of spatial correspondence with no evidence of a coherent field elsewhere.

Second, anchoring concerns where cells fire rather than how much. Firing rate differences between states were small and their sign depended on the measure: −0.090 in log₂ when rate was averaged over position bins, −0.028 when computed as spikes per second of running, and +0.036 when trials were additionally speed-matched (all p < 0.005, n = 6,517). An effect of this size that reverses under a change of denominator and a behavioural control is not a rate effect.

Third, under the gate a cell whose two clusters both fall on the same side of its null is labelled anchored on every trial or on none, and its correlation with the population axis is then undefined. These cells are 24.8% of the population in sessions where the state actually switches, and the split is identity-dependent and runs in opposite directions: putative interneurons locked on (109 always-anchored against 2 never, consistent across mice), non-spatial cells locked off (39 against 293), while grid and non-grid spatial cells were balanced. Non-grid spatial cells dominate both locked groups only because they are approximately 70% of the population, and they lock at their base rate.

Speed coding accounts for the locking, and does so in the direction the identity split predicts. A cell tuned to running speed has a profile that repeats on every trial, because the speed profile along the track is itself stereotyped; the classifier compares each trial's rate map against the cell's own template, so such a cell is called anchored on every trial and never varies. Entorhinal speed cells are predominantly fast-spiking interneurons, which is the class that locks on. Scoring speed modulation in the open field, so that it cannot have been shaped by the task dynamics being explained, the interneurons that lock on are markedly more speed-modulated than those that vary (median open-field speed score +0.141 against +0.065, p = 0.005; Figure 2I), and the same gradient holds in principal cells (+0.042 against +0.029, p = 8 × 10⁻⁵). The locked-on population is therefore not a separate phenomenon but the speed-coding population, seen through a classifier that asks whether firing repeats across trials.

Together, these data indicate that the population anchoring state is followed by grid cells together with local inhibition, rather than by spatial tuning in general, and that these two classes form a coordinated subnetwork rather than two populations independently reflecting a shared signal.

### Cells that follow the population state are enriched medially, within the superficial grid population

If grid cells and interneurons form a subnetwork, it should have an anatomical address. We localised neurons by reconstructing the position of each of the four shanks of every probe and registering recorded cells to a standardised atlas. The best-fit recording plane was fitted to device contacts rather than to cell positions, because per-cell mediolateral position is a per-shank nominal value plus jitter, and fitting to it would fit the approximation rather than the probe geometry (Figure 3B and C).

Grid cells were enriched medially within session (median ρ = −0.078 against grid membership, negative in 18 of 27 multi-shank sessions, p = 0.025) and superficially (8.9% of superficial spatial cells against 3.9% of deep, higher in 21 of 31 sessions, p = 0.002), whereas dorsoventral and anterior-posterior position predicted identity no better than chance (p = 0.90 and 0.10). The gradient is visible without a statistic: open-field rate maps from four sessions in one animal whose probes sat at approximately 3000, 3270, 3540 and 3720 µm, with grid and non-grid cells drawn in the ratio actually recorded, yield 10 grid cells of 24 at the medial end and 1 of 24 at the lateral end (Figure 3A).

Following was organised on the same axis and in the same direction, but testing this required care about which sessions can answer the question. Pooling cells across sessions produces a large gradient (medial 0.205 against lateral 0.120, ρ = −0.15, p = 2 × 10⁻²⁵), but pooling is not safe here, because probe placement differs markedly between animals — 16.5% medial in one, 63.1% in another — so a cell's mediolateral coordinate carries information about which session it came from. The test must be made within session, where every cell shares one probe placement, and on sessions that have mediolateral variation to test: position is assigned per shank, so a single-shank session contributes only noise, and 33 of 61 sessions span under 50 µm. Restricted to the 27 sessions spanning at least 200 µm, two shank pitches or more, the gradient held (median ρ = −0.109, negative in 19 of 27 sessions, p = 0.034). Dorsoventral position (p = 0.99, 59 sessions) and probe depth (p = 0.92) showed nothing, and for those axes the session-selection criterion made no difference, since every session varies along the shank — which indicates that the mediolateral result is specific to the axis rather than produced by the restriction. Layer behaved differently from the mediolateral axis: although it predicts identity, it did not predict following within session (median ρ = +0.028 against superficial membership, 25 of 39 sessions positive, p = 0.154; superficial +0.158 against deep +0.139 compared within session, 24 of 36 sessions, p = 0.077). Pooled across sessions the superficial bias in following does reach significance (+0.162 against +0.134, p = 0.004), but pooling is no safer here than it was mediolaterally, and the within-session tests are the ones we rely on. One multi-shank session shows the gradient on its own: agreement with the population axis fell monotonically across the shanks, 0.31, 0.53, 0.36 and 0.04 from medial to lateral (213 cells, 720 µm span, ρ = −0.255, p = 0.0016; Figure 3G).

Two caveats apply. First, because mediolateral position predicts identity and identity predicts following, the following gradient could be an expression of the identity gradient. Restricting to non-grid cells left the effect size almost unchanged (median ρ = −0.100) but it did not reach significance at 26 sessions (p = 0.111), so we report both and claim neither over the other. Second, given the curved shape of MEC and the flat plane of a four-shank probe, mediolateral organisation could in principle be a laminar sampling artifact. It partly is. Superficial layers supply 90.3% of entorhinal recording sites in the most medial quartile and 59.8% in the most lateral, a gradient present in every animal (within-mouse median ρ = −0.447, negative in 6 of 6, p = 0.031), and the same bias carries through to the cells analysed (Figure S5). Stratifying the mediolateral tests by layer separates the two results. The identity gradient does not survive it: within superficial cells the effect roughly halves and loses significance (median ρ = −0.051, p = 0.24 at 25 sessions) against −0.078 (p = 0.025) across all layers. The following gradient does survive, and is if anything stronger within superficial cells (median ρ = −0.144, p = 0.043, 22 sessions) than across all layers. Deep layers are sampled by too few sessions to test alone. We therefore treat the mediolateral gradient in following as the more robust of the two and the gradient in identity as partly confounded with layer — which reverses the order of confidence the first caveat above would suggest.

Together, these data indicate that the cells that follow the population state occupy a specific region of MEC. The mediolateral gradient belongs to following itself. The superficial bias belongs to identity: layer separates grid cells from other spatial cells but does not separate followers from non-followers within session, so the responsive subnetwork is medial, and lies within the superficially biased population that grid cells occupy, rather than being independently superficial. At this sample size we cannot separate an anatomical gradient in following from the anatomical gradient in identity.

### Monosynaptic connectivity does not account for shared anchoring

That grid cells and interneurons agree is a trial-level statistic and remains compatible with two populations independently tracking a common signal. Putative monosynaptic connections allow the same question to be asked in millisecond spike timing. We reasoned that if the anchoring state is propagated through the local circuit, then pairs of neurons that are synaptically connected should share the state more strongly than pairs that are not.

Connections were identified from cross-correlograms (Figure 4). Interneuron-to-grid was the strongest pathway in the dataset (3.93% of testable ordered pairs, 5.1-fold over within-session identity shuffling, p = 0.0005) and grid-to-grid the strongest excitatory one (0.217%, 2.6-fold, p = 0.004). Detecting the inhibitory half required extending the standard criteria, which test for a causal peak and therefore detect excitation only, onto the lower tail of the same Poisson test. Two features of the resulting connections indicate they are not an artifact of that extension: they are slower than excitatory connections (median 3.0 against 1.0 ms), and they originate from waveform-classified interneurons at 3.1 times their population share, neither of which the detector can produce by construction. The pairing seen in the trial-level statistics is therefore present in the wiring.

It nonetheless does not carry the state. Compared against unconnected pairs from the same session, of the same identity pair and at a similar probe distance (±40 µm), connected pairs shared the anchoring state no more strongly: grid–interneuron +0.176 against +0.187 (n = 193, p = 0.39) and interneuron–interneuron +0.090 against +0.090 (n = 116, p = 0.90). Both comparisons resolve effects of 0.045 and 0.043 at 80% power, approximately a third of the size of the identity effect they are testing against, so these are informative nulls rather than absence of evidence. The grid–grid comparison (n = 27, minimum detectable effect 0.157) is underpowered and we report it as such rather than as a null.

Together, these data indicate that anchoring travels with what a cell is rather than with what it is wired to. This is the result on which the rest of the paper turns: a state that is shared by cells of a given identity, but not more strongly by the subset of those cells that are synaptically connected, is unlikely to be propagated cell to cell within the circuit that expresses it, and is more consistent with a common input to which grid cells and local inhibition are more sensitive than other cells are.

### Transitions in the population state coincide with changes in attentional engagement

If the state is imposed from outside the local circuit, the question becomes what the population is listening to, and a global behavioural variable is the natural candidate. Pupil diameter is the standard non-invasive index of the arousal and engagement axis and was available on 80 of 81 virtual-reality sessions.

Pupil radius differed between the two states by approximately 0.24 SD and changed at transitions: entering the anchored state it fell by 0.414 z, and entering the non-anchored state it rose by 0.493 z (p = 1.7 × 10⁻⁷ and 4.4 × 10⁻⁸; Figure 5). Pupil radius did not change instantaneously, with the single trial spanning a transition accounting for only 16–31% of the total change. Because the classifier's median filter smooths each cell's label sequence over ±2 trials, these data cannot distinguish a genuinely graded change from an abrupt one whose onset is imprecisely timed, and we make no claim about which it is.

A constricted pupil is read as low arousal when it accompanies disengagement, and we therefore asked directly how pupil size relates to performance. Binning trials by within-session pupil z into deciles, the hit rate fell at every step from 0.826 in the smallest decile to 0.391 in the largest (14,011 trials, 58 sessions), and the anchored fraction fell with it. A mixed model gave −0.123 per z (p = 8 × 10⁻²⁷¹). Adding a quadratic term did not produce an inverted U: the quadratic coefficient was positive (+0.018, p = 3 × 10⁻¹⁵), placing the minimum of the fitted curve at z = +3.4, beyond the observed data, so that within the range the animal occupies the relationship is monotonically decreasing. The relationship was not a consequence of running: mean speed was flat across pupil deciles (37–40 cm/s) and controlling for within-session speed left the coefficient unchanged (−0.128, quadratic +0.011).

The shift is tonic rather than localised, and this is what identifies it as a change in engagement rather than attention paid at particular points on the track. Comparing pupil radius between states in each of 50 position bins, the difference has the same sign in every bin, reaches significance in 48 of them, and varies little along the track (mean -0.266 z, range -0.432 to -0.136, SD across bins 0.075 z). It does not trend with position (Spearman rho = -0.264, p = 0.064), and the reward zone is if anything slightly less affected than the rest of the track (-0.244 against -0.269). A state in which the animal attended selectively to task-relevant locations would concentrate the difference at those locations; instead the whole traversal is shifted.

Together, these data indicate that the anchoring state tracks a global behavioural variable indexed by pupil diameter, and that the anchored, constricted-pupil state is the one in which the animal performs. Because performance improves monotonically as the pupil narrows, we interpret this state as one of focused task engagement — a selective, exploitative mode — rather than as low arousal in the sense of drowsiness, which the behaviour excludes.

### The state is shared across structures but is read out most strongly by particular cell types

If the anchoring state is a global behavioural variable rather than an entorhinal computation, it should be legible outside MEC. Asking this properly means not making entorhinal cortex the frame of reference: scoring other regions against the MEC axis can only measure how much they resemble MEC and cannot ask whether they have a state of their own. Each region therefore built its own first principal component from its own cell × trial label matrix, and the axes were compared with one another (Figure 6).

We show the states themselves before the averages (Figure 6 supplement), for the two sessions that bracket the range among those recording all three structures. The block structure is shared and visible in the rasters and in single-cell rate maps without any statistic, while entorhinal–visual agreement runs from +0.82 down to +0.15, which is the context in which the population averages should be read.

Every region had a coherent state of its own. Split-half reliability — the axis built twice from disjoint halves of a region's own cells, size-matched to 8 cells per half so that MEC's hundreds of cells carry no advantage over visual cortex's tens — was +0.447 in MEC (27 sessions, p = 1.5 × 10⁻⁸), +0.474 in the subicular complex (10 sessions, p = 0.0039) and +0.330 in visual cortex (21 sessions, p = 2.4 × 10⁻⁵).

Before comparing these axes with one another we asked whether a single axis is an adequate description of a region's label matrix at all, since a first principal component exists whatever the data contain and its existence does not establish that the rest is noise rather than a second state. The natural statistic, the fraction of variance the first component explains, cannot answer this across regions: variance explained by the leading component of an n × T matrix rises as n falls, so a comparison between regions that contribute hundreds and tens of cells would largely report how many cells each contributed, in the direction that makes the least well sampled region appear the most one-dimensional. We therefore scored components out of sample, building the axis from one half of a region's cells and measuring the variance it explains in the other half, which a direction fitted to noise cannot do at any cell count (Figure 6 supplement 3).

Scored this way across 60 sessions, an axis built from 8 cells explained 0.052 of held-out entorhinal variance against a circular-shift null of 0.023, with an excess over null of +0.061 in the subicular complex and +0.020 in visual cortex. Comparing components at the same cell count, the excess was +0.0314 for the first and +0.0006 for the second, a ratio of approximately fifty, with the third to fifth at null. Because principal components are orthogonal by construction, a population carrying two independent states would have both generalise to held-out cells. We note that the second component does clear its null (p = 4.3 × 10⁻⁴), so the appropriate claim is that the label matrix is effectively rather than strictly one-dimensional. We note also that the null itself rises with cell count, because circularly shifted cells retain their own block structure and their leading component captures shared low-frequency structure that is estimated better as more cells are included, which is why components are compared against a shuffle null at matched cell count rather than against the value expected for a random direction.

Together, these observations indicate that a single axis is an adequate description of each region's anchoring labels, and that the population anchoring state is therefore a well-posed object rather than a convenient summary of something higher-dimensional.

Whether these are the same state cannot be read from a raw correlation between two axes, because such a correlation confounds a different state with no state: an axis estimated from few noisy cells is itself noisy, and will correlate poorly with anything. Dividing by the geometric mean of the two reliabilities separates them. Corrected this way, MEC and the subicular complex agreed at +0.920 and MEC and visual cortex at +0.863; neither differed from 1 (p = 0.098 and 0.515) and the two did not differ from each other (p = 0.66). The raw gradient, 0.46 against 0.31, therefore reflects how noisily each region estimates the state rather than which state it estimates. This correction amplifies noise by approximately 2.6-fold for the visual pair, giving a bootstrap confidence interval of [0.49, 1.23] with 8 of 19 sessions exceeding 1, so "not different from 1" excludes differences larger than about 0.3 and should not be read as a demonstration of identity.

What differs is the cell type rather than the region. At matched cell count MEC's own state was only marginally more coherent than visual cortex's (+0.417 against +0.334, paired within session, p = 0.065). Per cell, with every cell scored out of sample against an axis built from a random half of the entorhinal population, grid cells exceeded visual cortex by +0.149 (p = 9.9 × 10⁻⁶, 26 of 32 sessions) and interneurons by +0.144 (p = 1.3 × 10⁻⁶, 29 of 33), while other entorhinal cells exceeded it by only +0.050. The cell-type contrast is three times the regional one, and the regional one does not reach significance. Scoring out of sample is necessary here rather than leave-one-out, because leave-one-out removes a cell's own contribution but still measures an entorhinal cell against an axis built from its correlated network, whereas subicular and visual cells were never in that axis at all.

This also resolves an otherwise anomalous behavioural result. A population state built from visual cortex carries an Anchoring Dependence Index of +0.148 (p = 0.0014, 26 sessions), larger than the entorhinal state's +0.085 (p = 0.0015, 60 sessions), and the index is unchanged when the state is computed from MEC cells alone (p = 0.81). If the state is one global variable legible from any sufficiently sampled structure, this is expected rather than puzzling, and we therefore claim no behavioural specificity for the entorhinal readout. These session counts matter, because the same index computed on the speed-matched subset (Figure S3) reaches significance in MEC and not in visual cortex, which invites the opposite reading. That subset applies three requirements beyond the ones used here: at least 20 trials of each cue type, at least 10 trials in each anchoring state within each cue type, and at least 20 trials surviving the speed match with both outcomes still present. It therefore selects sessions that genuinely switch, and leaves 21 entorhinal and 10 visual sessions rather than 60 and 26. The mean index stays higher in visual cortex in both analyses (+0.148 against +0.085 here, +0.133 against +0.120 after matching); what changes is which reaches p < 0.05, and that follows from the loss of sessions rather than from a difference between regions.

Together, these data indicate that the anchoring state is global rather than entorhinal, and that the specificity lies in the cell type that expresses it: one state, present in every structure we recorded, read out most strongly by grid cells together with local inhibition. We note that the cerebellum, included as a distant structure with no parahippocampal connectivity that could explain a shared state, carries a matching state in both sessions in which enough cells were recorded to build an axis. It was intended as a negative control and is not one.

### The population state is associated with frequency-specific changes in the local field potential

If anchoring is a network state rather than a property of individual cells, it should be visible in the local field potential. We considered two possibilities. A uniform change in the amplitude of the field — arising from a change in the spiking contribution to the LFP, or from electrode referencing — predicts that power moves in the same direction at all frequencies. A change in the balance of inputs or in local processing predicts that it does not.

To distinguish these we computed Welch spectra per trial from the entorhinal LFP across the full range from 1.9 to 150 Hz rather than integrating over bands fixed in advance, because a band split whose "gamma" runs from 30 to 80 Hz cannot detect a change that reverses sign inside it. Spectra were whitened by multiplying power by frequency so that peaks are visible against the 1/f background, and z-scored within session so that no recording's absolute amplitude dominates. Across the 26 sessions with at least 8 trials in each state, the difference between states changed sign across the gamma range (Figure 7E and F): theta (6–10 Hz) fell by 0.094 (p = 0.020, lower in 20 of 26 sessions) and mid gamma (60–100 Hz) by 0.108 (p = 0.0056, 19 of 26), while slow gamma (30–48 Hz) rose by 0.105 (p = 0.0013, 21 of 26). Delta rose (+0.160, p = 0.036) and beta did not change (+0.063, p = 0.28). The pooled difference profile was positive to approximately 60 Hz and negative above it, with 25 of 26 sessions showing a crossing in that range at a median of 67 Hz (Figure 7J). Because the difference reverses sign, no uniform change in amplitude, referencing or spiking contribution can account for it.

We checked that the slow-gamma increase is not mains contamination, since the 50 Hz line falls within that band. Excluding the 48–52 Hz bins strengthened the effect (+0.109, p = 3.6 × 10⁻⁴) and the 48–52 Hz band tested alone did not reach significance (p = 0.063).

Theta's nesting of gamma changed for one band only. Quantified as Tort's modulation index over 18 phase bins with a phase-shuffle null subtracted, theta–fast-gamma coupling fell in the anchored state by 15.9% (p = 5 × 10⁻⁶, lower in 23 of 26 sessions), while theta–slow-gamma coupling did not change (−7.9%, p = 0.71, 11 of 26) (Figure 7G). The band whose power falls is therefore also the band that uncouples from theta, while the band whose power rises remains nested as before. We note that the slow-gamma null is the weaker of the two, since its absolute modulation index is approximately five times smaller and its band contains the mains notch, and we therefore report this as an unsupported difference rather than as a demonstrated invariance.

Although slow gamma rose and fast gamma fell in most sessions, we do not find that one is drawn from the other. The difference index (slow minus fast) was positive in 21 of 26 sessions (+0.213, p = 4.4 × 10⁻⁵), but the magnitudes of the two changes were uncorrelated across sessions (ρ = −0.08, p = 0.70), where a redistribution of a fixed amount of power predicts a negative correlation; and the 16 of 26 sessions falling in the slow-up and fast-down quadrant are no more than the two marginals already imply (15.3 expected, Fisher p = 0.59) (Figure 7H and I). The defensible statement is that both bands move in opposite directions, not that power is redistributed between them.

Theta amplitude also fell in the anchored state, although marginally (Δz = −0.224, p = 0.033 across the 31 sessions with speed-matched trials in both states; an amplitude fall of 1.66%, p = 0.032). This is not a shift of the rhythm out of the analysis window: instantaneous frequency was unchanged (−0.016 Hz, p = 0.54) and the spectral peak moved by −0.110 Hz (p = 0.016), far too small to move power out of a 4 Hz band.

The relationship between theta and behaviour changed more robustly than its amplitude. Theta amplitude and frequency both track running speed, and that coupling was approximately halved during anchored epochs: on the 19 sessions with at least 15 trials in each state, the within-session regression slope of theta amplitude on speed fell from 0.374 to 0.218 (p = 0.045) and of theta frequency on speed from 0.0126 to 0.0070 Hz per cm/s (p = 0.012), with the corresponding correlations falling from 0.424 to 0.244 (p = 0.0015, 15 of 19 sessions) and 0.398 to 0.185 (p = 3.4 × 10⁻⁴, 16 of 19). Mice do run more uniformly when anchored (speed SD 6.07 against 8.58 cm/s, p = 2.2 × 10⁻⁵), which attenuates a correlation on its own, but several observations indicate that this is not the explanation. The regression slope is insensitive to range restriction and fell by the same factor; the per-session change in correlation did not track the per-session change in speed variability (ρ = −0.01, p = 0.92); restricting both states to their common speed range left the slope difference intact (0.12 against 0.34, p = 0.008); and a circular-shift null on the state vector, which preserves run structure and within-session drift, gave p = 0.005. That the gain changes rather than only the level distinguishes this from a state in which the animal simply runs differently.

Because theta's coupling to speed changes while the rhythm itself does not, we asked directly whether the cells that carry the speed signal into entorhinal cortex change their speed coding with state. Entorhinal speed cells are reported to be largely fast-spiking inhibitory neurons that inherit a speed signal from the medial septum, so the prediction is specific: if the anchored state reflects a reduction in that septal speed pathway, the affected cells should be fast-spiking speed cells. We identified them entirely outside the virtual-reality session, from waveform class and the open-field speed score, and divided all entorhinal cells into four groups on those two criteria alone.

The classification reproduced the reported anatomy. Speed modulation was enriched approximately twofold among putative interneurons (58.7% against 41.5% of principal cells, odds ratio 2.01, p = 1.1 × 10⁻¹³), the median open-field speed correlation was three times higher in interneurons than in principal cells (p = 5 × 10⁻²³), and the relationship was continuous rather than categorical, with narrower waveforms and higher firing rates both predicting stronger speed modulation (ρ = −0.137 and +0.118, p < 10⁻¹⁸). The open-field labels also transferred to the task: on the virtual track, cells classified as speed interneurons in the arena had a median speed slope of 0.138 Hz per cm/s against 0.012 for non-speed interneurons, 0.010 for speed principal cells and 0.0008 for non-speed principal cells, a twelvefold separation obtained from a label assigned in a different environment.

Within those cells, speed coding did not change with the anchoring state. On the two states' common speed support, the speed slope of open-field-defined speed interneurons was 0.1523 when anchored against 0.1361 when not (+11.9%, p = 0.121, 29 sessions) — if anything steeper when anchored, which is the opposite of the predicted direction — and the rate-normalised gain was unchanged (+6.8%, p = 0.565). Neither of the other informative groups changed either (speed principal cells −22.2%, p = 0.121; non-speed interneurons −6.9%, p = 0.67). This null is informative rather than merely negative: the comparison resolves a 17% change in slope and an 18% change in gain at 80% power, against observed values of 12% and 7%, so a halving of speed drive of the size seen in the field potential is excluded in the cells that carry it. One group, non-speed principal cells, reached nominal significance (−39.2%, p = 0.026) on a near-zero baseline slope of 0.0023; across the sixteen comparisons made here, two results below 0.05 are approximately what chance provides, and we do not interpret it.

Speed modulation also did not predict how strongly a cell followed the population state. Pooled across entorhinal cells the two were weakly correlated (ρ = +0.066, p = 3 × 10⁻⁶), but entering both predictors in one model leaves cell class carrying the effect and speed modulation carrying none (putative interneuron +0.069, p = 1.2 × 10⁻⁶; speed modulation +0.005, p = 0.21). The pooled correlation is a composition effect: interneurons are both the most speed-modulated cells and the strongest followers, so pooling manufactures an association between two properties that are independent within class.

Finally, we asked whether discrete gamma events rather than continuous power carry the state or the task demand, reasoning that if gamma cycles correspond to discrete processing episodes then uncued trials, which require path integration, should contain more of them. Counting supra-threshold envelope excursions lasting at least one gamma cycle, expressed per theta cycle across 10,802 trials from 44 sessions, the raw comparison appeared to support this: fast-gamma events were more frequent on uncued trials (+0.0057 per theta cycle, p = 0.016). The effect did not survive control. Adding trial speed and theta frequency to the model reversed its sign (−0.0054, p = 4 × 10⁻⁵), and adding band power removed it entirely (+0.0003, p = 0.68). Event counts here track power and running rather than task demand, and we report this prediction as tested and not supported. The same analysis did reveal an organisation by track position: slow-gamma event rate peaked at 92 cm, within the reward zone, in 40 of 44 sessions between 70 and 130 cm, surviving control for speed and band power (+0.356 Hz for 80–110 cm against the rest of the track, p = 5 × 10⁻⁸), while fast-gamma events showed no such peak. This did not interact with cue condition (p = 0.93) or with anchoring state (p = 0.77), and so stands beside the state result rather than explaining it.

Together, these data indicate that the population anchoring state is accompanied by a spectrally structured change in the entorhinal field potential, in which theta and mid gamma fall while slow gamma rises, and by a reduction in the coupling of theta amplitude and frequency to running speed.

## Discussion

Our results indicate that anchoring of entorhinal spatial firing to a task reference frame is governed by a global behavioural state that the entorhinal circuit reads rather than builds. We find that anchoring is a coordinated property of the recorded population rather than of single neurons (Figure 1), that it is expressed by grid cells together with putative interneurons but not by spatial coding in general (Figure 2), and that these cells occupy a medial position within MEC, inside the superficially biased population that grid cells occupy (Figure 3). Although grid cells and interneurons are synaptically coupled, with interneuron-to-grid the strongest pathway we detect, synaptically connected pairs do not share the anchoring state more strongly than matched unconnected pairs (Figure 4), which argues that the coordination is not propagated through the local circuit. Consistent with the state being imposed from outside it, transitions coincide with changes in pupil-indexed engagement (Figure 5), the same state is present in every structure we recorded with only the strength of its readout differing by cell type (Figure 6), and it is accompanied by frequency-specific changes in the local field potential (Figure 7).

### Shared anchoring tracks cell identity rather than synaptic partnership

Coordinated switching among grid cells is usually interpreted through continuous attractor dynamics, in which recurrent connectivity enforces a shared network state. Our monosynaptic analysis does not support that interpretation for anchoring specifically. Two grid cells agreed at approximately the same level whether or not a synapse was detectable between them, and the grid–interneuron comparison would have resolved an effect a third the size of the identity effect it was testing against. We emphasise what this does and does not show. It does not show that the entorhinal circuit lacks attractor dynamics, nor that recurrent connectivity is irrelevant to grid firing; our sampling recovers only a small fraction of the connections present, and detectable monosynaptic connections are a biased and sparse sample of the circuit. What it shows is that, within the pairs we can test, synaptic partnership adds nothing to identity in predicting shared anchoring. The most economical account of that result is a common input to which grid cells and local inhibition are more sensitive than other entorhinal cells are.

The identity dependence is itself informative about the candidate input. Putative interneurons followed the state as strongly as grid cells and locked almost unanimously into the anchored mode, while non-spatial cells locked out of it. Neuromodulatory and septal projections to MEC target inhibitory populations preferentially, and a common input arriving through local inhibition would be expected to produce exactly this pattern: strong expression in interneurons and in the principal cells they most densely innervate, weak expression elsewhere.

The same dissociation appears a second time, with a different candidate property. Because entorhinal speed cells are predominantly fast-spiking interneurons, and interneurons are the strongest followers of the state, speed modulation is an obvious candidate for what the followers have in common. It is not: pooled across cells the two are weakly associated, but within class the association disappears entirely, and in a model containing both, cell class carries the effect while speed modulation carries none. What predicts a shared anchoring state is what a cell is, not whether it is synaptically connected to another follower and not how strongly it is tuned to running speed. Two functional properties have now been tested in place of identity and neither substitutes for it.

### A global state with a cell-type-specific readout

Asking whether the anchoring state is entorhinal required abandoning the entorhinal axis as the frame of reference. When each region was allowed to describe the state using only its own cells, every structure we recorded carried a coherent state, and once corrected for the reliability with which each region estimates it, those states were indistinguishable from one another. The regional difference that appeared in raw correlations was a difference in estimation noise rather than in what was being estimated.

We are cautious about how far this licenses a claim of identity. Disattenuation divides by the geometric mean of two reliabilities, which for the entorhinal–visual pair amplifies noise approximately 2.6-fold, so the comparison excludes differences larger than about 0.3 and no more. Sessions also differ substantially, with entorhinal–visual agreement running from +0.82 to +0.15 among sessions recording all three structures, and one session in which the two axes are anti-correlated. The claim the data support is that the state is not specific to entorhinal cortex, not that every structure expresses one identical variable.

The cerebellar result deserves comment because it was intended as a control and behaved as a finding. Cerebellar cells have no parahippocampal connectivity that would explain a shared anchoring state, and we included them expecting them to sit at zero. In both sessions with enough cells to build an axis, the cerebellar state matched the entorhinal one. Two sessions cannot establish this, and we report it as the weakest point at which the globality claim can be inspected rather than as independent support for it.

What remains entorhinal is the readout. The contrast between grid cells and visual cortical cells was three times the contrast between MEC and visual cortex as regions, and the regional contrast did not reach significance. On the behavioural side there is no entorhinal specificity at all: a state read from visual cortex predicted path integration-dependent performance at least as well as one read from MEC. We therefore make no claim that the entorhinal readout is what drives behaviour, and note that this is the expected result if a single global variable is legible from any sufficiently sampled structure.

### What the field potential adds, and what it does not settle

The spectral result constrains mechanism more than the amplitude result does. A change confined to overall amplitude would be compatible with many uninteresting explanations, including a change in how much spiking contributes to the field or in electrode referencing. That the difference reverses sign within the gamma range excludes those, because each acts in one direction at all frequencies. The accompanying loss of theta–fast-gamma coupling, with theta–slow-gamma coupling preserved, points in the same direction: the band that falls in power is the band that also uncouples.

We resist a stronger reading. Slow and fast gamma in the entorhinal–hippocampal system have been associated with different input streams and, in one influential framework, with internally guided retrieval and externally driven encoding respectively. An animal localising a reward zone by path integration is in the internally guided case, and that is also the condition in which anchoring matters most for behaviour, so the direction of our effects is the one that framework would predict for a shift toward internally guided processing in the engaged state. But those band assignments derive largely from hippocampal work, our recordings are entorhinal, and we have not shown that the two bands here carry the inputs the framework assigns them. We also note that the two gamma changes, although both reliable, are uncorrelated in magnitude across sessions, so we cannot describe them as a redistribution of power.

The decoupling of theta from running speed is robust to every control we applied but remains mechanistically unexplained. The most natural reading, that the velocity signal driving the entorhinal network is down-weighted when position is pinned by landmarks, predicts changes in the cellular speed code that we do not observe — and the sharpest version of that test, restricted to the fast-spiking speed cells that carry the septal speed signal and defined entirely from the open field, excludes a change a third the size of the field-potential effect in the very population the account requires it in. We tested the gamma-as-discrete-processing-episode hypothesis directly and it was not supported: apparent differences in event counts between cued and uncued trials tracked running speed and band power rather than task demand.

### Limitations

Three limitations bear on the central claims. First, the monosynaptic null is a null, and although both informative comparisons resolve effects a third the size of the identity effect, absence of a wiring contribution within detectable pairs does not exclude one within the much larger set of connections we cannot detect. Second, the non-entorhinal recordings sit on the same probe as the entorhinal ones, so for the field-potential analyses volume conduction cannot be excluded, and a shared effect was always going to be ambiguous where a divergent one would have been decisive. Third, the classifier's median filter smooths each cell's label sequence over ±2 trials, so our data cannot distinguish a graded transition from an abrupt one that is imprecisely timed, and the temporal structure of state transitions should be regarded as unresolved.

### Ideas and speculation

If the anchoring state is a global variable with a cell-type-specific readout, then the relevant question for grid cell function shifts from what the entorhinal circuit computes to what it is listening to, and when. One implication is that the availability of a usable grid code may be set by processes that have nothing to do with space. A mouse that is disengaged has a grid network that is not anchored to the task, and therefore cannot localise a reward by path integration, even though nothing about its spatial circuitry has changed. If this generalises, variation in path integration ability between individuals may reflect variation in engagement rather than in path integration machinery as such, which would be relevant to the interpretation of spatial deficits in ageing and in neurodegenerative disease.

A second implication concerns experimental design. Cue-rich virtual environments, which are widely used to study grid cells, engage behaviours that our results suggest can be solved without anchored grid firing. In such environments the anchoring state may vary without behavioural consequence, and grid activity recorded there may be only loosely coupled to what the animal is doing.

Finally, the identity of the common input remains open, and two septal candidates that seemed natural are now both constrained by the data. Medial septal GABAergic neurons are the obvious one, since their inhibition disrupts grid periodicity and phase precession together, but the direction of our field-potential result does not fit an account in which the non-anchored state is reduced septal drive: theta amplitude is lower in the anchored state, which is the state in which the code is best locked to the track. Spike–theta phase locking is also unchanged between states, which is what an intact pacemaker looks like.

That suggested a cell-type distinction, since the septal pacemaker and the speed signal reaching entorhinal cortex are carried by different projections, and a reduced speed–theta gain with preserved spike–theta timing resembles a change in the speed pathway with the pacemaker intact. The speed-pathway account is now also difficult to sustain. Entorhinal speed cells are predominantly fast-spiking inhibitory neurons, and we confirm that here; but those cells' speed coding does not change between anchoring states, and the comparison has the power to exclude a change a third the size of the one the field potential shows. If anything their speed tuning is marginally steeper in the anchored state, which is the wrong direction. The speed signal arriving on the cells that carry it is therefore preserved while its expression in the field potential is not, and the locus of the decoupling lies somewhere other than the afferent speed drive or the local cells receiving it.

A third septal projection is constrained by neither result. The medial septum and diagonal band send cholinergic as well as GABAergic and glutamatergic axons to entorhinal cortex, more densely to its medial than its lateral division, terminating in the superficial layers where the grid network and its feedback inhibition lie. Neither observation that narrows the other two candidates bears on this one: a change in cholinergic tone requires no change in the septal pacemaker, whose signature — preserved spike–theta phase locking — we observe, and no change in the speed signal arriving on the cells that carry it, which we also observe. It predicts the direction of the field-potential result rather than being embarrassed by it. Cholinergic tone drives theta, suppresses delta and favours fast over slow gamma, so a lower tone in the anchored state predicts lower theta, higher delta, lower fast gamma and higher slow gamma, which is the pattern we find; and because acetylcholine modulates gamma power rather than gamma frequency, it further predicts that the gamma peak should not move between states, which it does not.

Our pupil measurement is better suited to this reading than it would ordinarily be. Rapid pupil dilations track noradrenergic activity, whereas sustained dilations during locomotion are accompanied by sustained cholinergic activity (Reimer et al., 2016); our animals run throughout, and the anchoring state is a sustained variable held across blocks of tens of trials. The timescale fits for the same reason. Neuromodulatory tone varies over tens of seconds to minutes, which is the scale of the blocks we observe, whereas a state propagated synaptically within the entorhinal circuit has no particular reason to vary on that scale — a point independent of the connectivity null, which concerns which pairs share the state rather than how slowly it changes.

We offer this as the septal candidate our data do not exclude rather than one they establish. Pupil diameter indexes a correlate of cholinergic activity and not acetylcholine itself, and nothing here separates cholinergic tone from any other variable covarying with sustained engagement. The direction also runs against the familiar reading in which cholinergic activation accompanies attention: here the code is best anchored, and the animal performs best, when the pupil is narrow. Testing it requires measuring or manipulating cholinergic tone directly, which these recordings cannot do.

A different locus is suggested by what the two gamma bands are likely to be. Gamma rhythms are generated by fast-spiking inhibitory neurons, in loops whose frequency is set by the kinetics of the inhibitory limb, so two bands that move in opposite directions are more naturally two loops than one rhythm of varying amplitude. Our monosynaptic data describe such a loop directly: interneuron-to-grid is the strongest pathway we detect, grid-to-grid the strongest excitatory one, and the inhibitory limb is the slower of the two. The same fast-spiking population supplies the dominant inhibition onto grid cells, carries the speed signal, and generates the rhythms. That offers a reason for these cells to follow the state which does not depend on their coupling to grid cells: on this view they do not report the state so much as constitute it.

The state would then be the balance between a recurrent entorhinal loop and a feedforward sensory one, set by neuromodulatory tone rather than by either circuit, and the polarity is the one we observe. Cholinergic tone favours feedforward input over recurrent transmission, and the anchored state is the constricted-pupil state, which should therefore be the recurrent-dominant one: slow gamma higher, fast gamma lower and less tightly nested in theta. That vision should be down-weighted in the state where performance is best is not paradoxical in this task. When the gain between locomotion and the visual scene is altered, mice follow self-motion rather than vision (Tennant et al., 2018), and on uncued trials the reward zone carries no visual marker at all. The account also does not require the two bands to trade against one another, which matters because their magnitudes are uncorrelated across sessions: modulation of feedforward transmission and of recurrent gain act on different targets rather than redistributing a fixed amount of power.

We state this as a hypothesis rather than a conclusion, because its weakest step is an assignment borrowed from elsewhere. The association of slow and fast gamma with internally and externally guided processing derives from hippocampal recordings, where fast gamma indexes entorhinal input to CA1; that framework cannot be carried into entorhinal cortex unexamined, since there the same band cannot index its own input. The assignment could be earned directly in these recordings rather than assumed: in the sessions where entorhinal cortex, the subicular complex and visual cortex were recorded together, the two bands should differ in which structure they cohere with. Until that is done, the account identifies a locus consistent with our data rather than one our data establish.

We had expected phase precession to decide between the pacemaker and speed-pathway accounts, since they predict opposite outcomes for it. That test is not yet decisive in our hands. Precession is detectable in the expected direction — slopes are negative in 59% of cells (p = 3 × 10⁻⁹) — but at a magnitude far below what the phenomenon should produce, with a circular–linear correlation barely separable from the chance level for the available spike counts, and a minimum detectable state difference several times the size of the entire signal. Both the naive and the field-matched comparisons are null, and neither null is informative. We attribute this to pooling spikes across field traversals, which is close to worst-case in a dataset where field position varies between trials by construction; a per-traversal analysis is the obvious next step, and until it is done we draw no conclusion from precession in either direction.
